In July 1969, NASA launched Apollo 11, the first mission to land humans on the moon. It was the result of President Kennedy's 1961 challenge to Congress to get a man on the moon before the end of the decade. The vehicle that made it possible was the Saturn V, a three-stage rocket weighing around six million pounds at liftoff. Three astronauts sat at the top of it, Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin, and Michael Collins, and this is a bit about who they were before they got there.



Michael Collins sat on the right seat, where you are now. He had a wife and three children back on Earth waiting for him to come home. They knew he was going to space, but it is worth asking how much anyone on Earth could really picture what that meant. The blankness of it. When Collins was later asked if he was disappointed not to walk on the moon, his wife answered the question simply. She said she thought he was there with them in spirit.



Buzz Aldrin sat in the middle seat. He was operating as the lunar module pilot for the mission. To give some sense of the scale of what that meant, the Apollo VIII spacecraft had around fifteen million moving parts. A failure rate of just 0.1 percent would mean fifteen thousand parts not working. On that mission, only five failed. The Saturn V carrying these three men had even more components than that. Aldrin was not just flying a ship. He was operating something built and maintained by thousands of engineers on the ground, every part of it checked and rechecked by people who would never leave the atmosphere. His wife Joan and their children Mike, Jan, and Andy were among the many people on Earth watching and waiting.



Neil Armstrong sat on the left seat. He would be the first person to set foot on the moon, followed shortly after by Aldrin. Armstrong was by most accounts a quiet and private person, which fits with Collins' description of the crew as amiable strangers. His wife Janet and their sons Ricky and Mark were at home in Houston. He was headed toward a lifeless rock 238,900 miles away. When Janet Armstrong was later asked about the greatest moment of their life together, she did not mention the moon landing. She said it was when they were married.



But before any of that, before the first footsteps, the three of them were here. In this ship. They travelled for four days, six hours, and forty five minutes, further from Earth than anyone had ever been. The mission ran on the work of thousands of engineers, and on a significant amount of public hope. Outside the windows, the moon was getting closer. They had a job to do.
